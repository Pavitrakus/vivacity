```
Me

I am Pavitra Kushwaha. Sixteen. Kanpur mostly, DPS Azad Nagar, with chunks of my year dissolving into Delhi or Bangalore whenever the work stops fitting inside a school timetable.

Class four was the first computer in our house and I fell in love with it the dumb way, like opening a gift and refusing to put it down. A CS book said you could make games by dragging blocks around in Scratch so that became the whole after-dinner project, my first one was a messy space game where the ship kept exploding for no reason and I still stayed up fixing it. Mom thought I slept at normal hours. I did not. Fifteen days later I was weirdly fluent and when our school techno fair showed up I walked into competitions meant for older kids and somehow came first against class eight boys. I still cannot cleanly explain that day, but the feeling was electric. Something in my head went: okay, my life just chose a direction.

After that it was a climb. HTML in class five, Python next, Java by the time I hit eight. India locked down for COVID when I was in class six and while everyone else remembers boredom or fear I remember an absurd private paradise of coding until my eyes burned. By class eight I had shoved a fitness app onto the Play Store which my family politely installed and approximately nobody else did. That embarrassment was useful. Cute projects stop feeling cute when you want the world to actually move.

Class ten, 2024, I watched my grandparents crush Facebook reels all day and still freeze the moment they needed a cab or grocery order. The phone was entertainment, never a tool. So I built ORCA so they could boss the phone around through WhatsApp in ordinary language. That season was chaotic competitions, cold emails to researchers, a teenager hunting for proof he was real. Some of it was hunger. Some of it was validation theater. I am fine admitting both. ORCA taught me I could ship a full weird product end to end, and once that muscle existed the next problem I could not ignore was my own.

Then JEE prep in class eleven arrived and I kept hitting the same wall. When a problem stuck, I did not want another paragraph from a chatbot or a random forty minute lecture that only half matched my question. I wanted the diagram to move. I wanted the step that makes your brain click, the kind of click I never got the night electrostatics refused to sit still in my head. I dropped the exam path and decided if that video did not exist at a cost and speed a student could actually use, I would build the machine that makes it. That machine is Vivacity. I am the right person for it in a boringly personal way: I was the stuck kid, so I keep building for him.

Last summer I packed a half serious bag and flew to Bangalore with friends because waiting in Kanpur felt like cowardice. We slept wherever the math allowed, including two proud broken days outside a Shell petrol pump with laptops on our knees after a hackathon win while money was still “on the way.” It was ridiculous and I would do it again. Through Pratham Pengoria from JEE Simplified we got pointed at Ravneet Singh at ProLearn. We had no appointment. Security was correctly suspicious. Ravneet walked past the corridor, stared at three teenagers, and let us talk. He roasted what was soft in our thinking then later hired me into video pipeline work when we returned with demos, with a stipend and credits that help and still are nowhere near enough to thrash the engine thousands of times. We have early B2B conversations, early Physics Wallah walkthrough interest, IIT Bombay competition noise in the background. I work with Aditya Bhatia and Tanish Anand. I am writing this as the point of contact.

Mainstream consensus I agree with

Hard exams are good for serious kids even when they feel cruel in the moment.

Indian families already vote with years of their lives on JEE and NEET style gauntlets. The fashionable take that tough selection is meaningless never sat right with me. Pressure can be ugly and coaching can be extractive, and still the underlying idea that depth in math and physics should be earned by solving hard things feels right to me. What keeps me up is how many kids face that hard thing without ever getting a clean visual explanation for the exact doubt that is eating their evening.

Idea

Handmade 3Blue1Brown energy video is beautiful and slow. People write Manim by hand, fight LaTeX, record voice, stitch timelines, and for Indian platforms that cost explodes into either generic concept sims that miss your exact question or human clips that cannot answer a chatbot at midnight. Vivacity takes any topic or stuck question on earth and runs a pipeline that writes the teaching script, speaks it, drafts the Manim, renders scenes, patches code when a render dies, then returns a video in English or Hindi or the Hinglish kids actually speak. JEE and NEET doubt is where the wound was personal for me and where Indian distribution is loudest, but the engine itself does not care if you typed a parabola, photosynthesis, or a random history chain.

On my machine a two minute clip usually lands in three or four minutes when the stars align, with a unit cost that laughs at freelancer Manim rates if I am careful. Quality is the part I will not trade away. We still lean on Claude and OpenAI while we slowly grow a cheaper specialist model, and that training is not a cute two month side quest, so for now the bottleneck is boring: money to buy enough compute and API that we can run it until every ugly edge case climbs out of the woods. I want Vivacity inside the flows where Indian students already ask questions, so an app can answer with motion when someone is stuck at midnight.

I have been deep on this for months, school on one shoulder and product on the other. ProLearn keeps me near real production constraints. Creator conversations keep me near the JEE wound. The grant ask is $8000: about $3500 for cloud render time across half a year, $2500 for model and voice API, $1500 so I can keep showing up for demos in Bangalore and Delhi without inventing miracles, $500 for tools and mess. Stipend and family stretch cover surviving. Emergent Ventures money covers making Vivacity fail loud and get sturdy.

If you fund trajectory shifts, this one is simple. Give a sixteen year old with a working Manim video engine enough gas to burn the pipeline hard until reliability is the boring part.
```
